Part 1 · covers the measures

Four things worth counting, and one worth modelling

6 min live

Order feels like a matter of taste, which is why it never gets reviewed. It is not. Four properties of a sequence can be counted, and once they are counted the argument about order gets much shorter.

LiveWhy "words before the point" and not just position3 min▶

Position alone is misleading in both directions, which is why the bench reports both numbers side by side.

  • Slide 3 of 3 can be worse than slide 5 of 9. If the two slides in front are dense methodology, three slides is a longer road than five light ones.
  • Cutting slides does not always shorten the road. Merging two slides into one keeps every word and improves the position number, which is how a deck can look better and read identically.
  • It catches the thing people do when told to lead with the answer: they put the claim on slide one and then write two hundred words of setup underneath it.

The ladder below was derived from this bench in a browser before this page was written. If you turn the levers on in the same order you will get the same numbers, because they come from the same measurement.

LiveThe trap: "can you put a bit of context first?"5 min▶

This is the most reasonable-sounding request in corporate life and it is the single most expensive thing you can do to a deck. Turn on only the context lever, with nothing else, and the bench reports the damage on the original deck: the point moves to slide 6 of 6, words before it go from 142 to 203, and the claim count rises from 9 to 12.

  • It never replaces anything. Context slides are added, never swapped. Every one is pure addition to the road.
  • It arrives after the deck is finished, when there is no time to rebalance, so it goes on the front exactly as written.
  • It brings its own claims. The bench counts three on the context slide alone, and they are claims about process rather than about the finding.
  • It survives to the next version because deleting somebody's requested slide is a conversation and keeping it is not.

The answer is not to refuse. It is to ask which decision the context changes. If it changes none, it belongs in the appendix or in the two sentences you say out loud while the first slide is up - and saying it out loud costs the deck nothing at all.

Self-studyThe one lever that makes a number worse, on purpose4 min▶

Turn on only "write the claim on the visual", from the as-presented state. Words before the point go up, from 142 to 165, and the bench does not hide it.

That is correct and it is a real trade. Writing the claim on each visual adds words, and it buys something the word count cannot see: a deck that still works when you are not in the room to narrate it. Sessions 3 and 6 both depend on it.

  • Words before the point is a cost measure, not a quality score. A bench that only ever moved in the flattering direction would be teaching you to trust it, which is worse than teaching you nothing.
  • The trade is only worth it once the order is right. On the final state the same lever costs 4 words. On the as-presented deck it costs 23, because it is adding labels to two slides that should not exist.
  • This is the general shape of the thing: fix the order first, then spend words on clarity. Spending them first makes the wrong deck longer.

Open your own deck and find the point. Which slide first states your session 1 sentence. Write down N of M.

Count the words on every slide up to and including it. Titles, bullets, labels, captions. Most people are surprised by a factor of two.

Count your distinct claims. Every separate assertion, not every slide. If it is more than your slide count, session 3 is not finished.

Move the point to slide one and recount. The drop is your headline number and it is usually the largest single improvement available to you.

Now cut, in this order: slides serving no claim, then second claims on surviving slides, then words. Cutting words first is the common mistake and it saves the least.

Write the four numbers on a sticky note and put it on the deck. Re-measure before you send it.

If you take one thing from tonight. Reordering is free and cutting is cheap. Rewriting is expensive and it is what people reach for first, which is why decks get more polished without ever getting shorter.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Measure three real decks on the four numbers. Include one you received - it is easier and it calibrates you.
  2. Reorder the worst one and record the before and after. Reorder only; no rewriting allowed.
  3. Find a context slide somebody asked you to add and work out what it cost. Then decide whether it could be two spoken sentences instead.
  4. Take one deck and write its two-minute spoken version. Session 6 is about the same story in four different containers, and the spoken one is the hardest.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does the bench report words-before-the-point alongside the point's position, rather than position alone?

Both numbers are objective, so B is not it. The pairing exists because each one is gameable alone: merging two slides improves the position and keeps every word, and putting the claim on slide one with two hundred words of setup underneath improves position while the road stays exactly as long.

2 · Turning on only "write the claim on the visual" raises words-before-the-point from 142 to 165. What is the right conclusion?

A bench whose numbers only ever moved in the flattering direction would be teaching you to trust it rather than to read it. Labels cost words and buy independence from the presenter. Note the ordering lesson underneath: the same lever costs 23 words on the as-presented deck and 4 on the fixed one, because on the bad deck it is labelling slides that should not exist.

3 · Somebody asks you to add a context slide at the front. What is the best response?

Refusing turns a small thing into a disagreement, and adding it costs 53 words on the fixed deck and pushes the point to slide 6 of 6 on the original. The question about which decision it changes is the productive one: it usually reveals the requester wants reassurance the work was rigorous, which a spoken sentence supplies better than a slide.
